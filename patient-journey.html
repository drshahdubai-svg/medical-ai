End-to-end prevention and accountability

Patient Safety at Every Step

From planning a visit and entering the hospital to diagnosis, treatment, discharge and recovery at home.

Every transition creates uncertainty. A patient may be frightened, unable to find the correct department, unaware of a relevant medicine, waiting for an investigation or carrying incomplete records from another hospital. A safe system anticipates these problems rather than waiting for harm to occur.

Core principle: Prevention is patient safety. Clinical AI should strengthen verification, prioritisation, communication and timely decisions—not replace accountable clinicians.

This page combines professional experience and a proposed model workflow. Technology features described as opportunities are design proposals, not claims that an AI system has already been deployed or validated.

Stage 01 · Before and at the hospital entrance

Registration, reassurance, accessibility and correct identification

A patient arriving at a large hospital may be frightened, unwell, unable to walk comfortably or unfamiliar with the language and building. The first objective is to remove avoidable uncertainty while recognising urgent clinical needs immediately. Administrative speed is valuable only when it preserves safety.

1. Before arrival: make the visit predictable

Patient pathway: A patient books through a website, telephone service or assisted booking desk. The hospital confirms the department, clinician, appointment window, approximate location, registration requirements, payment/insurance documents and any preparation required. Where appropriate, the patient can complete demographic information and upload relevant referral letters, medication lists and prior reports before arrival.

Operational ownership: Appointment services maintain the schedule; registration verifies demographics; the clinical team sets preparation instructions and identifies referrals needing early triage. Digital registration is optional, not a condition for access.

Exception handling: A patient without internet access, an elderly patient, an unexpected visitor or someone who cannot read should receive telephone or face-to-face assistance. A patient reporting urgent symptoms must be directed to clinical triage, not told to wait for an ordinary booking.

Potential AI support: Explain preparation in the preferred language, identify missing administrative fields, forecast demand and propose appointment capacity. Any automated symptom-routing tool requires validation and a clear human escalation route.

2. At the entrance: a human welcome and immediate risk recognition

Patient pathway: A trained guide welcomes patients at a visible assistance point, asks which department they need and identifies requests for a wheelchair, mobility escort, interpreter or help with documents. Staff know how to summon clinical triage promptly when a patient appears acutely unwell.

Accessibility: Keep wheelchairs available and maintained; provide accessible entrances, lifts, seating and routes; offer escorts to those who are distressed, visually impaired or unable to navigate independently. Clear bilingual signs combine large text, department names, pictograms and colour-coded arrows. Colour is an additional cue, not the sole method of navigation.

Accountability: Facilities maintain accessibility; patient relations and guides manage navigation; nurses or other qualified clinicians perform clinical triage. Guides must not make clinical diagnoses.

What could fail? A patient with chest discomfort may be misdirected to a distant outpatient counter; a person with limited mobility may be left waiting for assistance; a language misunderstanding may send the patient to the wrong department. These require visible escalation routes and staff training.

3. Multiple registration counters: remove the single bottleneck

Patient pathway: Patients may register online or at department-specific counters, staffed kiosks or assisted mobile stations. Every station connects to the same master patient index so that distributing registration does not create duplicate records. An arrival confirmation directs the patient to the correct clinic and queue.

Safety checks: Verify identity using at least two accepted identifiers; check for an existing hospital number; confirm spelling, date of birth and contact information; reconcile duplicate or conflicting records before attaching orders or reports. Emergency care is not delayed for financial or administrative completion.

Queue design: Monitor arrival rate, processing time, counter availability and the proportion of patients requiring assisted registration. Reassign staff or open overflow desks when demand rises; provide a staffed exception desk for identification or insurance problems.

Potential AI support: Forecast queues and identify unusually long waits. Human supervisors decide staffing changes, and any prediction system must be checked for unfairly disadvantaging walk-ins, disabled patients or those needing language assistance.

4. Patient card, identification and optional RFID coordination

Patient pathway: Once verified, the patient receives a card or wristband linked to the hospital identifier. Scanning at the consultation room, laboratory, imaging department and pharmacy can confirm arrival and support tracking of outstanding orders. RFID may help coordinate location and patient flow where a lawful, secure and proportionate programme exists.

Clinical safeguards: Staff independently verify two identifiers before collecting specimens, performing procedures, giving medicines or issuing reports. A location signal is not proof of identity, consent or clinical readiness. Patients should understand what data is collected and who can see it; apply access controls, retention limits and alternatives where appropriate.

Escalation: Lost card, unreadable tag, mismatched record or duplicate registration triggers manual identity verification and correction before non-urgent downstream actions continue.

5. Financial navigation without fragmenting clinical care

Self-pay pathway: The hospital may offer an optional prepaid balance—for example, a patient deposits ₹10,000 and receives itemised deductions for authorised services. Patients must be able to see charges, receive receipts, challenge discrepancies and obtain refunds of unused balances under clear terms. A card is a payment convenience, not a clinical identifier on its own.

Insurance pathway: Verify coverage, pre-authorisation and co-payment requirements through a separate financial workflow. The patient receives understandable explanations of covered and non-covered services. Payment questions should not prevent urgent triage or necessary emergency stabilisation.

Operational safeguard: Clinical orders, authorisations, payment transactions and refunds must be auditable and kept distinct from the clinical decision itself.

Stage-one review indicators: Time from arrival to initial guidance; time to clinical triage for urgent presentations; registration waiting-time distribution; duplicate-record incidents; wheelchair/interpretation response time; wrong-department arrivals; patient-reported navigation difficulties; payment disputes. Targets should be set from local baseline data, not invented as universal standards.

Stage 02 · Outpatient operations

Appointments, walk-ins, consultation and investigations

6. Appointments and walk-ins: two arrival routes, one safe triage standard

Scheduled patients: Confirm attendance and route patients to the correct clinic. Design schedules using actual consultation lengths, clinician availability, procedure requirements and reasonable buffers for complex cases.

Walk-ins: Register through an available department or overflow desk; ask a qualified triage professional to identify urgency and determine whether same-day review, another appropriate clinician or a later appointment is safe. Explain the options and expected wait instead of leaving the patient uninformed.

Clinical priority: Time-critical symptoms take precedence over booking order. Patients whose condition changes while waiting need a straightforward route for reassessment.

Operations: Use live information on clinician availability, queue lengths, examination rooms and delayed appointments to redistribute demand. Keep communication proactive: a delay that is explained is different from an unexplained wait.

Potential AI support: Forecast arrivals, highlight bottlenecks and suggest appointment adjustments. Do not let a scheduling algorithm override clinical triage.

7. Before the doctor enters: prepare the clinical record

Confirm the presenting complaint, relevant symptoms, current medicines, allergies, previous visits and referral documents. Ask about past illnesses, operations, family history, exposure or community risks and prior investigations where relevant. Mark unverified outside records as unverified; do not silently merge contradictory diagnoses.

Documentation fidelity and structure: Record accurately what the patient actually says, distinguishing direct patient statements from clinician interpretation, third-party accounts and previously documented information. Organise the history under consistent headings: presenting complaint; history of present illness (onset, course, associated symptoms and pertinent negatives); past medical and surgical history; family history; community, occupational and exposure history; medicines and allergies; systems review; examination; investigations; assessment and differential diagnoses; management and follow-up. Record the source and date of externally supplied information. Never invent negative findings or treat an unanswered question as a negative response.

Responsibility: Nurses and other trained staff may gather structured information within their scope; the treating clinician verifies the accuracy and completeness of clinically important documentation, examines the patient, considers differential diagnoses and documents the reasoning and plan.

AI opportunity: Identify missing fields or conflicting medicines for clinician review. AI-generated summaries must retain provenance and uncertainty and must not invent normal findings or imply a history was taken when it was not.

8. Consultation: the decision must follow the evidence

The doctor correlates the patient's account, examination, relevant past and family history, risk modifiers, red flags, prior records and investigations. The record should explain the working diagnosis, important alternatives, reasons for ordering or not ordering tests, treatment choices, safety-net advice and planned follow-up.

Failure to prevent: Premature diagnostic closure, missed anticoagulants or renal disease, unrecognised infection exposure, unsupported certainty and prescriptions issued without necessary medication reconciliation.

AI evaluation: Assess both documentation accuracy (does the record faithfully reflect what the patient, family and clinicians actually said?) and documentation structure (are findings organised into clear, retrievable clinical fields?). Then test whether the AI separates facts from assumptions, identifies missing decision-critical information, recognises contradictory entries and prompts clarification rather than offering a falsely confident answer. A well-formatted note can still be inaccurate; an accurate but unstructured narrative can still conceal critical information.

9. Investigation orders: guide, schedule, perform and close the loop

Once a clinician authorises an order, the electronic system routes it to the appropriate laboratory or imaging service with patient identifiers, clinical indication, urgency, required preparation and relevant precautions. A guide or clear directions take the patient to the service. Where multiple suitable locations exist, the coordination team may select the less congested one without compromising clinical priority or expertise.

At the test site: Reconfirm identity, match the order to the patient and specimen or imaging protocol, check required preparation and contraindications, and explain what happens next. For contrast examinations or other higher-risk tests, relevant clinical screening remains essential.

Result ownership: The requesting clinician or an explicitly designated covering team must review results, communicate significant findings and act on critical values. A pending-result list should identify who follows up when a patient leaves before a report is finalised.

When something goes wrong: A missed appointment, failed sample, unavailable scanner, delayed report or critical result requires a recorded action, responsible owner and escalation deadline based on clinical urgency.

10. From consultation to pharmacy without an unnecessary second queue

An authorised electronic prescription is transmitted to the outpatient pharmacy immediately. The pharmacy may check stock, screen the order and prepare labelled medicines before the patient arrives. Preparation does not remove the pharmacist's responsibility to clarify interactions, contraindications, allergies, dosing or unexplained medication changes.

Collection: Recheck identity, confirm the final authorised prescription, complete payment or insurance processing and offer counselling in a nearby private area. The pharmacist uses teach-back and explains when to seek help. A patient with a discrepancy should be routed back to the prescriber, not told to guess which medicine to take.

Stage-two review indicators: Scheduled and walk-in waiting times separately; triage-to-clinician time by urgency; consultation interruptions; investigation turnaround and repeat-test rates; unreviewed or uncommunicated results; pharmacy prescription-to-ready time; counselling completion and patient understanding; complaints and near misses. Review safety alongside speed: faster throughput alone is not proof of better care.

Stage 03 · Clinical documentation, reasoning and information integrity

Document what was said. Structure it. Verify it. Then reason.

Clinical reasoning begins with the fidelity of the clinical record. Two independent questions must be answered: Is the documentation an accurate account of what was communicated? And is that information organised into a clear, clinically useful structure? Neither quality can be assumed from the other.

11. Capture the patient's account faithfully

The clinician or authorised recorder documents the presenting complaint in the patient's own terms where clinically useful, the onset and progression of symptoms, their severity, associated features and what the patient believes is happening. Distinguish direct statements from the patient's interpretation, observations by family or carers, previous records and the clinician's own assessment. Use professional interpretation support when needed and identify the source of translated information.

What can go wrong? A statement such as “I have not noticed any blood” may be incorrectly converted into “no bleeding”; an unanswered question may be recorded as “negative”; speech recognition may confuse medication names, dose units or negation. Correct these discrepancies with the patient before relying on the record.

AI evaluation: Compare source dialogue or source notes with the resulting documentation. Score omissions, additions, altered timing, invented findings, medication transcription errors and reversals of meaning. Clinical note fluency is not evidence of fidelity.

12. Use a consistent clinical documentation structure

Organise the record so that important information can be located and reviewed reliably. A practical structure includes:

  • Presenting complaint and history of present illness: chronology, symptoms, associated features, relevant positive and negative findings.
  • Past medical and surgical history: chronic conditions, previous admissions, operations and complications.
  • Family history: inherited and familial disease risks when relevant.
  • Community, social, occupational and exposure history: contacts with infectious illness, travel, local outbreaks, environmental exposures, smoking, alcohol and functional circumstances as relevant.
  • Medicines and allergies: names, doses, frequency, actual use, recent changes, adverse reactions and source of verification.
  • Examination and observations: findings actually elicited, with times and relevant vital signs.
  • Investigations: ordered, pending, received, reviewed and clinically interpreted results.
  • Assessment: working diagnosis, alternatives, supporting and opposing evidence, uncertainty and risk modifiers.
  • Plan and handover: treatment, escalation, monitoring, patient explanation, responsible clinician and follow-up.

Preventive safeguard: Structured fields should improve retrieval and verification, not force the clinician into false precision. Allow clinically meaningful free text, explicitly mark unknown or not assessed, and avoid copying forward stale findings as if newly verified.

13. Reconcile records from another hospital

At transfer or consultation, identify the source institution, dates, diagnoses, treatments, medicines and tests. Compare the outside record with the patient's account and current findings. The receiving team owns the safety of current decisions, while the previous institution remains responsible for the accuracy of records it generated.

Verification process: List missing or conflicting items; classify them by potential impact; request relevant original reports, images, discharge summaries and medication administration records through authorised channels; document the request, contact, response and designated follow-up owner.

When records cannot be obtained: Do not assume an unavailable result was normal. Re-examine the patient, repeat or substitute a clinically necessary test where appropriate, and defer non-urgent high-risk decisions if essential information is absent. Urgent stabilising treatment should not wait solely for administrative retrieval.

14. Establish the complete medication timeline

A final discharge prescription does not reveal all medicines the patient previously used or those given and stopped during admission. Reconstruct the sequence: before admission → started in hospital → changed in hospital → temporarily withheld → intentionally stopped → intended at discharge. Identify discrepancies and obtain a prescriber decision where clinical judgement is required.

Sources may include the patient or carer, medication containers, pharmacy dispensing history, referral letters, inpatient administration records and prior discharge summaries. Document which sources were checked and which details remain unverified.

Clinical risk example: Before recommending an NSAID, verify relevant renal disease, anticoagulants, gastrointestinal bleeding risk and other contraindications. If essential facts are unknown, seek them; do not present an unsafe prescription as definitive.

15. Reason explicitly from evidence to diagnosis

Once the record is verified as far as practicable, identify the main clinical problem, red flags and risk modifiers. Develop a proportionate differential diagnosis, recording findings for and against important alternatives. Order investigations to resolve meaningful uncertainty rather than merely confirm an early assumption.

Decision review: Does the diagnosis follow from the history, examination and tests? Were dangerous alternatives excluded appropriately? Were missing investigations acknowledged? Does the management plan match the level of certainty and urgency? Does the patient understand the explanation and next steps?

Decision-support checkpoint: When the working diagnosis is entered, offer a quiet side-by-side, source-linked review of clinically important alternatives and evidence. The clinician decides whether to accept, investigate, defer or reject a material suggestion, with proportionate documentation.

AI evaluation: Test separately for documentation fidelity, documentation structure, reasoning fidelity, uncertainty recognition and decision safety. Attribute failures accurately: source-record errors, transcription or summarisation errors, clinician reasoning errors and AI reasoning errors are not interchangeable.

16. Close the documentation loop with ownership

Every critical finding, outstanding investigation, unresolved medication discrepancy and pending outside record needs a named clinical owner, action and appropriate review timeframe. Ensure handovers state what is known, what is uncertain, what must be checked and what should trigger escalation.

Audit measures: Source-to-note discrepancy rate; clinically significant omissions; unverified copied findings; completeness of structured histories; unresolved medication discrepancies; outstanding results without assigned ownership; time to retrieve decision-critical records; and safety events linked to documentation or communication.

Evaluation rule: Do not confuse an incomplete source record with an AI reasoning failure. However, a safe AI should recognise missing decision-critical information, communicate uncertainty and request clarification. This documentation gate supports the existing ten rubrics and sixteen error labels; it does not create a new rubric.

Cross-cutting safety layer · Every diagnostic decision

Side-by-side, source-linked evidence review with clinician accountability

Whenever a clinician records a working or final diagnosis, or reviews an investigation, a non-obstructive decision-support panel can show important alternatives, relevant supporting and conflicting findings, missing decision-critical information and links to the underlying source records. The responsible clinician makes the final decision.

This is a proposed workflow and research hypothesis, not a claim that AI alternatives are always correct or that the interface has been clinically validated.

Clinician's entry

Working diagnosis, imaging interpretation, laboratory interpretation or management conclusion, together with the clinician's reasoning and the source of the information.

Source-linked review panel

Relevant alternatives; findings for and against; red flags; medication and risk modifiers; missing or contradictory records; direct links to source passages, observations, images or reports; and a clear indication of uncertainty.

A. Trigger at each diagnostic checkpoint

Offer review when a diagnosis or consequential interpretation is entered, changed or signed: outpatient consultation, emergency assessment, inpatient review, ultrasound, radiography, CT, MRI, laboratory interpretation, pathology where supported, and discharge diagnosis. The review must use the actual clinical context and be tailored to the specialty and decision.

Workflow safeguard: Do not generate a distracting interruption for every trivial entry. Use an unobtrusive panel for ordinary alternatives; reserve interruptive alerts and required acknowledgement for sufficiently validated, time-critical risks. The system must not autonomously alter the diagnosis or block emergency stabilisation.

B. Present evidence, not an unsupported verdict

Show the clinician's proposed conclusion beside AI-identified alternatives, each with its supporting or conflicting source evidence. Distinguish verified clinical facts, patient-reported facts, inferred possibilities and unavailable information. The interface should make it easy to inspect the actual note, test result or image region. A confident AI statement without supporting evidence should not be promoted as a correction.

Example: For an older patient unable to bear weight after a fall, a normal initial hip X-ray may be shown alongside the persistent clinical concern for an occult fracture. The panel should link to the documented inability to bear weight and the actual radiology report, and prompt appropriate reassessment rather than automatically replacing the clinician's diagnosis.

C. Record the clinical response without transferring responsibility to AI

Where a material suggestion is displayed, preserve an auditable record of the system version, underlying data available at the time, suggestion text, source links, clinical priority, display time and whether the clinician acknowledged it. Record whether the clinician accepted, investigated, deferred or rejected the suggestion and, for consequential overrides, the clinical rationale.

Important distinction: A record that a suggestion appeared does not prove the clinician saw, understood or was obliged to follow it; nor does it establish that the AI was correct. Audit data supports review, learning and governance. Clinical and legal accountability depends on the circumstances, institutional policy and applicable standards—not merely on an override log.

D. Extend the review across departments

  • Clinical diagnosis: alternative diagnoses, relevant negative findings, risk modifiers, red flags and missing history.
  • Ultrasound: consultant checks the acquired images, technical adequacy and junior report; AI may identify a possible finding or missing view, but cannot confirm findings absent from the acquired study.
  • CT and MRI: radiologist reviews AI-suggested findings against images, prior studies and clinical indication, then signs the final report.
  • Laboratory investigations: identify critical values, trends, possible specimen or unit problems and discordance with the clinical picture; verify before acting.
  • Medication decisions: compare the prescription with allergies, renal function, anticoagulants, prior therapy and reconciliation status.
  • Discharge: compare final diagnoses, outstanding investigations, medication changes and follow-up instructions with the longitudinal record.

E. Reduce unnecessary interruptions and review time

Prioritise only decision-relevant discrepancies, group related suggestions, suppress repeated unchanged low-value prompts, show concise evidence summaries with direct source access and distinguish routine review from urgent escalation. Monitor alert fatigue and false-positive rates. A critical finding requires an explicit escalation pathway to an appropriate responsible clinician rather than relying on passive display alone.

F. Research question and evaluation design

Question: Does showing source-linked, decision-critical information alongside an AI summary improve detection and correction of dangerous omissions compared with the same summary alone?

Proposed pilot: Use fictional clinical cases with frozen AI summaries: some accurate, others containing clinically consequential omissions or unsupported assertions. Compare first-pass review of the summary alone with review using a compact source-linked evidence panel. A single-clinician pilot can identify failure modes and assess feasibility, but cannot establish generalisable improvements in clinician performance.

Outcomes: Consequential errors correctly identified and corrected; final decision safety; review time; false alarms; unnecessary rejection of correct summaries; confidence and any new mistakes introduced by the panel. The hypothesis remains unproven until tested.

Governance rule: Source information must first be accurate and structured. The AI review should help reveal uncertainty and alternatives, while the treating doctor or responsible reporting specialist retains authority over the final decision. This cross-cutting layer applies throughout the patient journey and supports the existing R1–R10 rubrics and sixteen error labels without changing their definitions.

Stage 04 · Diagnostic imaging

Review the images—not merely the written report

6. A supervised ultrasound reporting hub

In a model based on the user's described departmental improvement, junior doctors or sonographers work in nearby rooms while an experienced consultant supervises reporting from a central review area. The consultant should be able to review relevant saved images and, when necessary, assess the examination directly or request additional views before signing off.

The failure to prevent: a junior examiner may fail to obtain or recognise a finding, and a senior reviewer may sign a written report without sufficient image verification.

Proposed AI role

The side-by-side evidence panel should display image-linked alternatives and the evidence for each suggestion, with an audit trail of material alerts and the consultant’s response.

AI may flag potentially abnormal findings, image-quality concerns, missing required views or discrepancies between the images and draft report. It must not be treated as proof that an unacquired image is normal. The responsible consultant verifies findings, resolves disagreement and signs the final report.

7. CT, MRI, radiography and other investigations

Extend the same principle to other imaging services: correct patient and examination, appropriate protocol, adequate image quality, preliminary interpretation, comparison with prior imaging and specialist review.

  • AI-generated preliminary observations should be visibly labelled as provisional.
  • Prioritise potentially time-critical abnormalities for rapid radiologist review.
  • Display the image region and supporting evidence behind each AI prompt, where technically feasible.
  • Record disagreements, corrections and final clinician decisions for quality improvement.
  • Require a clear pathway for unexpected or critical findings to reach the treating team.

At sign-off, the reporting radiologist should be able to review relevant AI-suggested alternatives and supporting image regions in the source-linked panel; consequential disagreements should be traceable without treating the AI suggestion as authoritative.

Reducing unnecessary review burden

Use concise, confidence-calibrated prompts, clear image localisation, prioritised worklists and one-click access to relevant prior studies. Avoid repeated low-value alerts and unvalidated auto-sign-off. Measure time saved, missed findings, false alarms and diagnostic accuracy before claiming efficiency gains.

Stage 05 · Outpatient pharmacy

Prepare medicines early; counsel before the patient leaves

8. Electronic prescription to pharmacy preparation

Once the treating clinician authorises an electronic prescription, the pharmacy can receive it immediately and begin stock verification and preparation while the patient walks from the consultation area. Preparation should not mean automatic dispensing: clinical screening and final identity checks remain essential.

  • Check allergies, interactions, duplicate therapy, dose, route, duration and relevant renal or other risk modifiers.
  • Resolve unclear or unsafe orders with the prescriber before dispensing.
  • Provide a single clear collection point and transparent billing information.
  • Allow patients to use an optional prepaid account or card with an itemised balance; offer ordinary payment methods and a separate insurer authorisation workflow.
  • Never permit insufficient prepaid funds or insurance delays to obstruct urgent stabilising care.

AI opportunity

Flag clinically significant prescribing discrepancies and predict dispensing workload. The pharmacist and prescriber remain responsible for clinical verification and resolution.

9. Dedicated outpatient medicine counselling

Place a counselling room beside the dispensing area, staffed by an outpatient pharmacist. The patient should understand why each medicine is prescribed, how and when to take it, important interactions, expected effects, warning signs and whom to contact if problems arise.

Verification: Use teach-back rather than simply asking whether the patient understands. Provide instructions in an appropriate language and accessible format. Check that the patient knows which previous medicines to continue or stop.

Stage 06 · Admission and inpatient care

Prevention continues across wards, theatres and critical care

Ward pharmacists

Reconstruct the best possible medication history, review every inpatient addition, omission, hold and dose change, and support reconciliation at transfers and discharge. Verify the right medicine, patient, dose, route, timing and duration.

Infection prevention

Hand hygiene, aseptic practice, device review, appropriate antimicrobial use, surveillance, isolation precautions where indicated and patient education.

Operating theatre safeguards

Correct patient, procedure, side and level; consent; marking; active team time-out; equipment readiness; antibiotic timing when indicated; intraoperative communication and safe handover.

ICU and step-down

Appropriate monitoring, recognition of deterioration, escalation, daily review of level-of-care needs, safe transfer criteria and avoidance of unnecessary prolonged critical-care stays.

Pain and recovery

Timely analgesia, reassessment, adverse-effect monitoring, mobilisation and functional goals, with adjustments for individual risks.

Ethics and governance

Informed consent, confidentiality, incident reporting, root-cause analysis, corrective action, controlled-medicine security and emergency preparedness.

Stage 07 · Transition home

Discharge is a handover, not the end of care

10. Multilayered discharge safety and accountability

The proposed verification sequence reflects the user's described multidisciplinary discharge process:

  1. Ward pharmacist: compare pre-admission, inpatient and proposed discharge medicines; clarify what was started, stopped, withheld or changed.
  2. Nursing team: verify practical instructions, wound care, administration education and patient understanding.
  3. MBBS duty doctor: review current status, discharge documentation and unresolved immediate clinical issues.
  4. Postgraduate specialty assistant: verify specialty-specific diagnosis, investigations, treatment rationale and follow-up.
  5. Treating consultant: make or confirm final clinical decisions, resolve outstanding risks and sign off the discharge plan.

Roles should be specified in local policy; each professional must document discrepancies and escalate unresolved matters rather than assuming a later reviewer will discover them.

11. Reconcile every medication transition

A discharge list containing only the most recently administered medicines is not enough. Compare the pre-admission regimen, inpatient administration record, temporary holds, intentional discontinuations, dose changes and final intended home treatment. Explain changes to the patient and communicate them to the next clinician.

12. Patient understanding and seven-day follow-up

Hello Apollo — Care Beyond Boundaries extended the safety process through structured telephone follow-up during the first seven days after discharge. Calls addressed fears, misunderstandings, medicines, wound concerns, warning signs, contact routes and the need for reassessment.

The aim is not merely a completed discharge document, but a patient who understands the plan and knows when and how to seek help during recovery at home.

Stage 08 · Clinical AI assurance

Where AI fits—and where human responsibility remains

Evaluate clinical AI at three distinct points: input integrity (was the record complete and verified?), reasoning fidelity (did the system interpret available evidence and uncertainty correctly?) and decision safety (was the resulting recommendation safe and accountable?).

Input and documentation

Missing history, community exposure, prior records, medication reconciliation, absent results and contradictory diagnoses.

Clinical reasoning

Differential diagnoses, supporting and opposing findings, risk modifiers, red flags and the limits of available evidence.

Decision and handover

Safe prescribing, appropriate investigations, escalation, consultant sign-off, patient communication and follow-up.

Performance measurement

Missed critical findings, false alerts, clinically meaningful corrections, review time, escalation delays and patient outcomes. Proposed AI benefits require prospective validation.

This journey is an overarching clinical workflow, not an eleventh rubric. It complements the existing R1–R10 evaluation framework and sixteen error labels. AI prompts are decision support, not an independent final diagnosis or authorisation to prescribe.
